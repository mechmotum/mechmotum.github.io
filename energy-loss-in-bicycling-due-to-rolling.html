Energy Loss in Bicycling Due to Rolling

Summary

Gravity, air, and rolling can all contribute to slowing a cyclist, but the energy loss due to rolling is not as simple as a traditional rolling resistance view makes it seem. The roads a cyclist traverses have varying degrees of roughness. When the loaded tire rolls over these rough surfaces it deforms and loses energy due to hysteresis, but the roughness also causes the entire bicycle-rider system to vibrate. These vibrations are partially absorbed by the tire, the bicycle materials, suspension systems, and the rider themselves. Each of these cause energy loss which translates to slowing the forward motion of the cyclist. When riding on high roughness road surfaces, the majority of energy loss can be from the vibration. This project is focused on developing the science behind this vibrational energy loss mechanism and seeking ways to reduce it.

Findings

Dijkman 2024

MSc Kirsten Dijkman first developed "De Veeger", our rolling loss measurement trailer which allows us to collect on-road data without air resistance and can be pulled over varied road surfaces. See it in action below:

De Veeger: floorless trailer designed for wind-free on-road power measurements.

In her thesis, she showed that you can find differences in tires but those difference are small for ~1 bar change or 2 mm tire carcass diameter. The uncertainty in our measurements needs to be reduced significantly to have reliable comparisons among such similar tires. She showed that paver bricks have higher losses than asphalt, as expected.

Power and speed from two runs over paver bricks using the same tire with the runs being in opposite directions to see the effect of gravity. The power losses with gravity accounted for amount to about 33 Watts in this case.

Vázquez 2025

MSc student Eloy Vázquez Fernández created a simulated cobblestone surface based on equivalent bicycle roughness index (BRI) and extracted vibrational losses using accurate speed measurements finding vibrational losses being dominant at higher speeds and accounting for 150-350 watts loss when seated in an aero-position on the bicycle.

Power loss due to vibration, rolling, and aerodynamic drag at 30 km/h seated over cobblestones for varying tire pressure.

He also found that vibrational losses do not behave linearly with respect to speed and tire pressure over this surface, showing that losses can be reduced by adjusting tire pressure depending on the speed.

Nonlinear surface of power loss due to vibration at different speeds and tire pressures while seated.

Bongers 2026

MSc student Quinten Bongers developed our variable surface treadmill and power loss measurement technique based on accurately measured towing force.

His measurements over a 3D printed paver brick surface with ~2 cm gaps aligned with Eloy's findings, in that the vibrational power loss is high (150 watts at 30 km/h) but Quinten found the speed, pressure, power loss relationship to be more linear for this surface.
